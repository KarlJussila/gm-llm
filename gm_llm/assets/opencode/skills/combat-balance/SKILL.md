# Combat Balance — difficulty by feel, for a solo game

Combat difficulty here is a **craft judgment, not XP math**. Two faces, same levers at different
depth: a heavier **planning** face (shaping an encounter to an intended feel) and a lighter **runtime
gut-check** (sizing up an unplanned fight at the table).

## Why not CR

CR and the encounter-budget system are calibrated for a ~4-PC party. This is a **single-PC game**, so
a lone PC against a group is action-economy hell — it eats N attacks a round and deals one character's
worth back. That, not any creature's CR, is the dominant variable. Don't reach for CR; size the fight
with the levers a feel-DM actually uses.

## The levers

1. **Action economy first.** For a solo PC, enemy *count* dominates individual power. Three same-tier
   minions is dangerous; one big creature is often *safer* than several small ones of equal budget,
   because the PC's own economy competes evenly against one.
2. **Two-way rounds-to-kill.** Roughly how many rounds for the enemies to drop the PC, vs. for the PC
   to clear the enemies' total HP? First ≤ second and no out → the PC probably goes down. Quantify it
   against the PC's actual sheet at planning; eyeball it at the table.
3. **Burst and save-or-die swing.** Can the enemies focus-fire to spike the PC in a single round? A
   lone failed save vs. a control/incapacitate effect is far swingier than HP loss — weight it heavily.
4. **The PC's outs and resource state.** A "deadly" fight with an escape route or a fresh PC is
   survivable; a "moderate" one in a sealed room against a depleted PC isn't. Difficulty is contextual.
5. **Shape with design levers, not a target number:** enemy count, HP/damage, starting distance,
   terrain, reinforcement timing, and the **objective** (kill-them-all vs. survive-three-rounds vs.
   reach-the-door). Starting distance and objective often shape a fight more than the stat blocks do.

## Planning face

Shaping an encounter (from `encounter-plan`): pick the intended feel, then set the levers to hit it,
and quantify the two-way rounds-to-kill against the PC's real numbers. **Telegraph danger in the
fiction** so a hard fight reads as hard *before* the dice. Bake any out into the *design* — an escape
route, a lull, a reachable objective — never into a reflex to rescue the PC once the dice turn.

## Runtime gut-check

Unplanned combat at the table — three questions, fast: **count the enemies and their action economy;
can they drop you faster than you can drop them; is there an out?** That is the whole check.

## Lethality is real

A fight turning against the PC is not a cue to fudge. Enemies act on their nature — an intelligent one
presses or flees as a thinking creature would, a cornered beast doesn't — and sometimes that means the
PC dies. If a fight is lethal, that is allowed: **intentional and telegraphed, never accidental, never
rescued.** The escape valve is design-time levers and emergent NPC behavior, not a PC-rescue reflex.
